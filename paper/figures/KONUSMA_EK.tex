\paragraph*{Second persons inside quoted speech.}
\vekalet{\textbf{In the raw-continuation outputs the second persons are not in someone else's mouth.} Running the chat-template outputs' check on the raw-continuation ones---quoted spans, speaker labels and dash-dialogue, and the complement clauses of speech verbs---puts $2\%$ to $6\%$ of the base outputs' second persons inside such a span (median $4\%$). \textbf{The chat-template outputs are not one figure but two regimes}: thirteen of its sixteen models put essentially none of their second persons after a fabricated turn marker, and the three whose templates write that boundary in plain text put $47$--$61\%$ there --- so the raw-continuation outputs' few per cent is read against a distribution with a floor at zero, not against a single chat-template number. \textbf{Read outside those spans the withdrawal is unchanged: $16$ of $16$ down and interval-disjoint.} A deliberately over-cutting ceiling reading, which also discards everything after the first blank line and so throws away ordinary paragraph breaks, leaves $15$ of $16$ (the exception is Qwen2.5-32B, whose interval then covers zero). \textbf{Two limits of this reading, named}: the detector under-counts --- straight-quote dialogue and any speech verb outside our list are missed --- so the in-span share is a \emph{lower} bound on exactly the quantity we want small; and because $M_1$ is a density, excising a span removes tokens as well as second persons, so the direction of the change is not conservative by construction. \textbf{So we measured it}: the excision removes $3$--$7\%$ of the tokens and \textbf{raises $M_1$ in all $16$ models}, which means the spans it removes are \emph{less} second-person-dense than the prose around them --- the second persons are not concentrated in the dialogue.}
