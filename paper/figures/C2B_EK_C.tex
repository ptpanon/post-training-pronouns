\paragraph{Addressed or generic ``you'': two judges against one labeller.}
\vekalet{The counters cannot tell a ``you'' addressed to the reader from a generic one (\S\ref{sec:measure}). A judge, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct at temperature 0, labelled $2{,}000$ second-person sentences drawn across the sixteen models and both checkpoints as addressed to a particular reader, generic, or neither (a quotation, invented dialogue, or undeterminable). One labeller, one of the authors, answered the same three-way question for $100$ of those sentences, drawn by model; the package named neither the model, the checkpoint nor the judge's label. The two agree on $76$ of $100$ (model-clustered $95\%$ interval $[0.70, 0.83]$), Cohen's $\kappa=0.55$ $[0.43, 0.67]$, against a chance agreement of $0.47$. The disagreements sit on the boundary the counters cannot see: $11$ sentences the labeller read as addressed the judge called generic and $6$ went the other way, and of the $7$ the labeller left undetermined the judge agreed on $3$. Agreement is $43$ of $55$ on aligned outputs and $33$ of $45$ on base outputs. We had fixed, before comparing the two, that the judge's split would enter the main text only at an agreement of at least $0.80$; it falls short. The judge is a Qwen2.5 model, like six of the sixteen. A second label judge (Claude Sonnet~5, same model family as the assistant) was tried after the first missed the bar; its agreement on the same 100 items is $84/100$ (model-clustered $95\%$ interval $[0.79, 0.89]$, $\kappa=0.69$ $[0.62, 0.78]$, chance agreement $0.48$; on the same sentences it matches the labeller $46$ times among aligned and $38$ among base checkpoints; the two judges agree with each other on $83$); its labels are used only where that bar is met. Both judges were compared with the labeller on these same 100 sentences, so the second was chosen on them. It meets the bar, and it then labelled all $2{,}000$ sentences under the same prompt, three times with a majority label. With its labels, the addressed rate (the second-person rate times the addressed share of its cell) falls from base to aligned on raw continuation in $8$ of 16 models and rises in $8$ (\S\ref{sec:measure}); with the open-weight cross-check judge's labels it falls in $11$. The generic rate, the second-person rate times the generic share of its cell, falls in $16$ of $16$ and rises in none; $12$ of the falls have a prompt-clustered $95\%$ interval below zero and $8$ exceed their paired placebo, a split of the base checkpoint's outputs into two halves within each prompt ($200$ splits). Labels were assigned under a three-question rule: (1) a quoted, fictional, or template- or prompt-artefact sentence is UNDETERMINED; (2) a ``you'' that refers to something this interlocutor said, asked, did or has is ADDRESSED; (3) otherwise, a ``you'' that can be replaced by ``one'', ``anyone'' or ``people'' without changing the meaning is GENERIC. The rule was fixed after cell 40 and cells 1--40 were re-read under it. After the second label judge had been chosen, the same labeller answered the same question for $30$ further sentences drawn from the same $2{,}000$, none of them among the $100$: the two agree on $28$ of $30$, the open-weight cross-check judge and the labeller on $23$, and the two judges on $25$. At this size we read the totals only: the package's own frame lists agreement per class and per checkpoint as unmeasurable here, and a $95\%$ interval on a rate of this kind spans about $\pm 0.14$. The cue classifier of Appendix~\ref{app:D1} labels $17$ of the same $100$ sentences. Its generic label matches the labeller in $5$ of $8$ and its addressed label in $6$ of $9$ (model-clustered $95\%$ intervals $[0.25, 1.00]$ and $[0.31, 1.00]$), and the labeller read $78$ of the $83$ it leaves undetermined as addressed or generic.}
